A customer who waits two days for a quote has usually booked someone else by the time it arrives. We lost 14 of 40 enquiries that way in one month, and every one of them had asked a simple question.

## The step that looks slow is rarely the slow one

When a small firm decides to automate quotes, it usually starts with the quote itself: a template, a price list, a form. That feels right. Writing a quote is the visible work.

But in our own inbox the writing took about 20 minutes. The wait was everywhere else. Enquiries sat unread for 9 hours on average. Then we waited a further day for the customer to send photos or measurements we had forgotten to ask for. The quote was the fastest part of the chain.

Automating the fast step made us faster by 10 minutes. It did not win back a single job.

## Three numbers to pull from your inbox

You do not need software for this. Take the last 30 enquiries and write down three times for each one.

1. **Time to first reply.** From the moment the message arrived to the moment anyone answered. Ours was 9 hours. The firms that won the jobs we lost replied within 1 hour.
2. **Back-and-forth count.** How many messages passed before you had what you needed to price the job. Ours was 4. Every extra round added about half a day.
3. **Time to quote after the last answer.** This is the step most tools automate. Ours was 20 minutes.

If the third number is the biggest, automate the quote. In our case it was the smallest by far.

## What we changed instead

We did not buy a quoting tool. We changed two things.

First, every new enquiry now gets an instant reply that asks the three questions we always need: the address, two photos and the preferred date. That cut the back-and-forth count from 4 to 2.

Second, one person checks the inbox at 9am, 1pm and 5pm, not "when there is time". First reply dropped from 9 hours to under 3.

After six weeks we were losing 5 of 40 enquiries instead of 14. The quote template did not change at all.

## Why this matters more than the tool

It is tempting to buy a tool and hope it fixes the whole chain. It will not, because the tool only sees the step it was built for. The numbers show you the chain.

Start with the 30 enquiries. Count the three times this week, and pick the biggest one to fix first.
